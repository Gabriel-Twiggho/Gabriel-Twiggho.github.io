Line-of-Sight Persistence

From unusable to Reliable and Deployable

Previous Best
40%
Our Solution
94.41%

The Problem

The Real-World Context

This problem is critical in applications like law enforcement pursuing a suspect vehicle, or disaster response teams tracking a drifting vessel, where persistent tracking is paramount.

The Gap in Existing Research

The best Line-of-Sight score from existing research was only 40%. At this reliability, the swarm loses sight of the target more than half the time. For real-world applications like search and rescue or surveillance, this simply isn't usable. A target that disappears 60% of the time isn't being tracked.

That's why we set out to develop a solution capable of achieving 90%+ LOS, a threshold where persistent tracking becomes viable for practical deployment.

Problem Constraints

  • Track a fast-moving, non-evasive target with a slow-moving swarm
  • Agent speed = 50% of target speed (2:1 speed disadvantage)
  • Agents can only communicate with K nearest neighbours
  • Score averaged over multiple target paths

Methodology

Core Hypothesis: No single behaviour can achieve 90% LOS. We need an adaptive solution.

1. Build the Framework

Created a 2D simulation optimization framework in Python, allowing rapid prototyping and testing of swarm behaviours.

2. Ideate

Explored various tracking and searching algorithms for the swarm, drawing from existing research and novel approaches.

3. Optimise & Test

  • Tested and optimised every combination of existing algorithms
  • Evaluated one-state, two-state, and three-state strategies
  • Built a Python framework to iteratively tune algorithm variables. Each algorithm had handfuls of parameters to optimise (e.g., memory duration, prediction multipliers)

4. Exploratory Design

Observed weaknesses in the simulation and designed entirely new, purpose-built behaviours to fill those strategic gaps.

The Solution: Three-State Architecture

The breakthrough came from a three-state solution, with distinct algorithms for when an agent is in front, behind, or has no memory of the target:

1. In Front → Aggressive Prediction

Prediction worked well all-round, but we observed that a more aggressive predict multiplier could be used when in front. Standard prediction sometimes caused agents to miss the target's path. The aggressive variant kept agents positioned on the target's trajectory.

2. Behind → Predictive Pursuit

Testing showed prediction was the best-performing behaviour when behind the target. Agents use a predictive multiplier to anticipate where the target will be, rather than chasing where it was.

3. No Memory → Inverse Square Repulsion

When an agent loses sight of the target, separation kicks in using inverse square repulsion. This forces agents to spread equally far apart across the map, maximizing sensor coverage for reacquisition.

Results

94.41%
Line-of-Sight persistence score
Previous Best: 40% → Our Solution: 94.41%

By moving from static behaviours to dynamic role allocation, we demonstrated that in resource-constrained, decentralized systems, adaptability is more powerful than raw speed.

Online Task Scheduling under Uncertainty

During my exchange semester at Loyola Marymount University in Los Angeles, I was invited by Professor Jared Coleman to join his research team. Even after returning home to Australia after the exchange I still work remotely with Professor Jared and his team helping where I can.

The research we do focuses on the question, "How do you schedule tasks on a supercomputer when you don't know how long they will take?"

Specifically the research linked below was focused on creating an automated framework for the SAGA Python library that adapts offline heuristics (like HEFT and CPoP) for online execution. By simulating a feedback loop that re-calibrates the schedule as tasks finish, we enabled static algorithms to handle runtime uncertainty dynamically.

Read the Publication Download PDF

Task graph visualization from our publication showing computational dependencies and scheduling complexity

Understanding the Task Scheduling Problem

Task scheduling aims to assign computational tasks to machines to minimize execution time while respecting dependencies. Traditional algorithms assume known task durations, but real-world systems face uncertainty from hardware variations, network conditions and workload unpredictability. Our research adapts classic scheduling heuristics to handle this uncertainty in real-time, making offline algorithms work effectively in dynamic, online environments.

Space Robotics Lab · Mechanical Design

Portable Vacuum Chamber

Built for the Space Robotics Lab, this compact vacuum chamber was built so that we could carry a dust-covered glass sample to a laser lab for experiments into whether laser energy can remove dust from a surface under vacuum.

Engineering journey

Design Workflow

These are the five steps that I took for the design workflow of this vacuum chamber.

01

Sketch

I first brainstormed the initial design through sketches on my phone since its a simple rectangular design establishing the chamber shape, flange position and overall sample layout.

02

SolidWorks

I then moved the concept into CAD to resolve the assembly, dimensions and clearances between components.

03

Prototype

After Cadding up I made a prototype (3D-printed model started off with a paper mache) The main purpose was so that I could reason with the workshop how to manufacture the design so that I could sensecheck it.

04

Fabrication

The design was reviewed with the workshop, then manufactured and assembled in metal.

05

Finished Chamber

The completed assembly was connected to the vacuum system and tested ahead of the laser experiment.

Iteration under test

Design Mistake & Recovery

01

Height and flange problem

The completed chamber and glass-flange assembly ended up slightly taller than intended, revealing a stack-up mistake on my end in the original design. Part of the glass flange was machined down to recover the height but the change affected the flange and introduced a vacuum leak. A water-pressure test located the leaking area and after discussion with the workshop, resin was applied to repair the seal. The recovery reinforced the importance of checking the complete flange stack-up before fabrication and validating changes before machining finished components.

02

Vacuum pump-down problem

After the local leak was repaired, pump-down testing exposed a separate system-level limitation. We wanted to simulate the lunar environment for that testing at approximately 10−3 mbar is "good enough" for research purposes but the overall system could only get to 10−1 mbar without continuous pumping. Individual parts were investigated finding that the entire system couldn't stay at the desired pressure, The practical conclusion was to move the pump to the laser laboratory and run it during the experiment. This broader limitation was distinct from the repaired flange leak.

Thermal Vacuum Chamber Control System

I helped turn an existing thermal vacuum chamber with heat shrouds and LN₂ cooling into an automated system. An operator could set a target temperature while the controller coordinated telemetry, heaters and cooling through a desktop HMI.

From manual plant to integrated control

System Architecture

The new architecture connected the chamber's thermocouples to ESP32-based heater and LN₂ control, with a desktop HMI for monitoring and operator commands.

Functional prototype → engineered final hardware

Hardware Development

The temporary wiring was intentional: it let me prove temperature acquisition, switching and integration before committing the design to a dedicated PCB.

01

Bench Prototype

Temporary electronics validated the control architecture while I wired the heat shroud, routed internal thermocouples and prepared an ESP32-based system to read temperatures inside the chamber.

02

The interface displayed telemetry, it started as a browser-based HTML HMI to a native desktop HMI built with PySide6. as multiple issues came up with the previous solution.

05

Thermal testing → model-based logic

Developing the Temperature Controller

The project aimed for a 1–2°C/min ramp and ±2°C hold, chosen to follow the NASA thermal-vacuum testing specifications we were working from. The first physical controller reached approximately the ramp-rate target but held around ±5°C. I then developed a model-based improvement from recorded data, although I could not physically validate that final controller before leaving the project.

01 · Thermal testing

Measure the chamber

Open-loop heating trials at different starting temperatures exposed the chamber's lag and showed why fixed heater cut-off temperatures could not provide reliable control.

02 · Initial control

Feedback with prediction

The early physical controller managed the ramp using feedback and temperature prediction, but still varied by about ±5°C around the final setpoint.

03 · System identification

Build a thermal model

Experimental data produced a four-pole transfer-function model with approximately 95% fit on the cleaner training data.

04 · Final control logic

Separate ramp and hold behaviour

The identified model was used to tune separate logic toward the project's NASA-aligned aims: a 1–2°C/min ramp and ±2°C hold.

Validation status: developed from recorded TVAC data, but not physically validated before the project concluded because other chamber hardware was still being worked on.

From a Basic CPU to a PID Processor

Goal of the hackathon was to design and build a custom CPU from the ground up, from its instruction set and datapath to its Verilog implementation, simulation and FPGA synthesis.

Once we understood the basics of CPU architecture, we developed the narrative of designing it for PID control.

Build & results

From processor fundamentals to PID control

Before this hackathon, we could write software, but the internal operation of a processor still felt like a black box. We had never followed a hexadecimal instruction from instruction memory, through the decoder and register file, into the ALU or understood how load and store operations connect a processor to data memory.

During the challenge, we learned those concepts by building the processor ourselves. Starting with a basic arithmetic CPU, we gradually implemented its datapath, instruction decoder, registers, memory interfaces and assembler.

Once the architecture made sense, we adapted it for closed-loop control. Signed fixed-point scaling, comparison and conditional branching allowed the processor to calculate proportional, integral and derivative terms, make runtime decisions and constrain actuator outputs.

We compared six CPU variants through functional simulation and FPGA synthesis, validated 1,000 PID updates and met the 50 MHz timing constraint.

The main problem for us, and this is also mentioned throughout the video below, was that PID requires fractional numbers, and implementing that in a CPU that fundamentally only works with binary integers can initially be challenging. It turns out the solution is quite ingenious: scale all the numbers up by a power of two so the fractional values can be represented as integers, perform the multiplication, and then scale the result back down, making sure that the hardcoded ISA we had knew about this scaling.

Ultimately, we built a CPU that could run a 1,000-iteration PID loop. Nothing more.
